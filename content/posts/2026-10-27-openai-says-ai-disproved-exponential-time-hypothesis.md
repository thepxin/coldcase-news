On 2026-10-26, OpenAI announced three new discoveries in mathematics, computer science and physics, concerning harmonic analysis, complexity theory and quantum field theory ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/building-scientific-understanding-with-academic-communities/)). The company states that its models proved the local smoothing conjecture in every dimension and that its system proved the Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH) false ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/building-scientific-understanding-with-academic-communities/)). It reached out to external experts and asked them to act as shepherds, organizing groups to understand the new results ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/building-scientific-understanding-with-academic-communities/)).

## Details (as Reported)

OpenAI describes the manuscripts as a form of raw research data and states that the shepherds advised three different release strategies ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/building-scientific-understanding-with-academic-communities/)). For the math result, the AI output was released and researchers are free to organize their own efforts to explain it ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/building-scientific-understanding-with-academic-communities/)). On the computer science result, an external group explains the work while the AI output stays public ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/building-scientific-understanding-with-academic-communities/)). In physics, an external group defers to its own judgment on releasing materials ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/building-scientific-understanding-with-academic-communities/)).

The Verge reported that the ETH paper gives a deterministic algorithm that exactly counts satisfying assignments of fixed-width CNF formulas in subexponential time and polynomial space, and that the 3-SAT case refutes the hypothesis ([The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/1019143/openai-eth-kaseya-science-math-physics)). OpenAI notes that ETH has been used in hundreds of papers to derive conditional hardness results, and that finding better algorithms will require significant further research ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/building-scientific-understanding-with-academic-communities/)). Impagliazzo and Paturi are original proposers of the hypothesis, and Williams has refuted strong versions of it ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/building-scientific-understanding-with-academic-communities/)). The group asked that all raw data be made public, and OpenAI agreed to fund a conference on the implications ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/building-scientific-understanding-with-academic-communities/)).

The collaborations follow four principles: transparency, no academic credit for OpenAI, recognition for the groups, and independence ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/building-scientific-understanding-with-academic-communities/)). Participants sign no nondisclosure agreements, and participation is voluntary and unpaid ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/building-scientific-understanding-with-academic-communities/)).

Engadget reported that the announcement follows a release of manuscripts with over 700 pages covering 372 major math problems, and that the physics group provided an email address for questions about its efforts ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2297941/openai-new-discoveries-mathematics-computer-science-physics/)).

## What Is Not Yet Confirmed

- OpenAI states that it does not yet know which of the three release approaches will be most useful ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/building-scientific-understanding-with-academic-communities/)).
- The ETH work is still to be verified and explained by the Impagliazzo, Paturi and Williams group ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/building-scientific-understanding-with-academic-communities/)).
- The physics information has not been released in full ([The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/1019143/openai-eth-kaseya-science-math-physics)).
- The AGMAI panel of math advisers recommended that AI companies not adopt the practice of designating certain people to receive output first, citing unequal access, borrowed reputation and biased selection ([The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/1019143/openai-eth-kaseya-science-math-physics)).
