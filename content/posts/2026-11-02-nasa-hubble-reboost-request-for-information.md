NASA released a Request for Information (RFI) on Thursday asking commercial companies for ideas on how to reboost, service or carry out a controlled disposal of the Hubble Space Telescope ([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/10/think-you-can-save-the-hubble-space-telescope-nasa-wants-to-know/)). Responses are due November 30 ([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/10/think-you-can-save-the-hubble-space-telescope-nasa-wants-to-know/)). The agency is not committing to buying a rescue mission ([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/10/think-you-can-save-the-hubble-space-telescope-nasa-wants-to-know/)).

NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman described the aim in a post on the agency's site: "Hubble still has important science ahead of it, and we should be looking at every reasonable opportunity to keep it delivering for as long as possible" ([The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/science/1024239/nasa-commercial-space-save-hubble)).

## Details (as Reported)

NASA is seeking information about companies' current and near-term capabilities for capturing Hubble and boosting it to an altitude of at least 373 miles (600 km), enhancing the telescope's performance, or guiding it to a controlled reentry ([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/10/think-you-can-save-the-hubble-space-telescope-nasa-wants-to-know/)). In the RFI, NASA wrote that "the boost and/or servicing mission must not harm the HST and that this mission is expected to occur by 2032" ([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/10/think-you-can-save-the-hubble-space-telescope-nasa-wants-to-know/)). The contractor would provide the launch vehicle, servicing spacecraft, mission operations and "crew if applicable" ([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/10/think-you-can-save-the-hubble-space-telescope-nasa-wants-to-know/)).

Hubble lacks onboard propulsion ([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/10/think-you-can-save-the-hubble-space-telescope-nasa-wants-to-know/)). NASA last raised its orbit in 2002, when the Space Shuttle Columbia visited on a servicing mission ([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/10/think-you-can-save-the-hubble-space-telescope-nasa-wants-to-know/)). The final shuttle servicing flight in 2009 did not reboost the telescope ([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/10/think-you-can-save-the-hubble-space-telescope-nasa-wants-to-know/)). It took 24 years for Hubble to glide from 362 miles (583 km) in 2002 to its current orbit ([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/10/think-you-can-save-the-hubble-space-telescope-nasa-wants-to-know/)).

Without a boost, the observatory will fall back into the atmosphere in the early-to-mid-2030s ([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/10/think-you-can-save-the-hubble-space-telescope-nasa-wants-to-know/)). The Verge reported that Hubble is in no immediate danger of burning up ([The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/science/1024239/nasa-commercial-space-save-hubble)). Isaacman added that "if there are innovative and cost-effective ways to extend the life of an asset already on orbit, we owe it to the American taxpayer and the scientific community to explore them" ([The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/science/1024239/nasa-commercial-space-save-hubble)).

## What Is Not Yet Confirmed

- NASA is not committing to buying a Hubble rescue mission ([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/10/think-you-can-save-the-hubble-space-telescope-nasa-wants-to-know/)).
- Officials want to know who might be interested in mounting a mission, how much it would cost, and which companies could reach Hubble before reentry ([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/10/think-you-can-save-the-hubble-space-telescope-nasa-wants-to-know/)).
- Orbital dynamicists currently forecast reentry in 2033 or 2034 without intervention, and the forecasts can change based on solar activity ([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/10/think-you-can-save-the-hubble-space-telescope-nasa-wants-to-know/)).
